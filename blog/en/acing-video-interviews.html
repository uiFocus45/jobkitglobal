Acing Video Interviews

Set up your space, master the camera, and handle every glitch with confidence

Published November 24, 2026 · 3 min read

Video interviews look easier than in-person ones: no commute, no waiting room, your notes within reach. Yet many candidates lose points on things that have nothing to do with their skills: a dark face, a frozen connection, eyes glued to the screen instead of the camera, or a recruiter who simply cannot hear them well.

The good news: almost everything that goes wrong on video can be prevented with a bit of preparation. This guide walks you through the setup, the on-camera habits that build trust, the special case of recorded interviews, and exactly what to say when technology lets you down.

What changes on video (and what doesn't)

The questions are the same as in a face-to-face interview, and so is the goal: proving you can do the job and that people will enjoy working with you. What changes is how your message travels. On a small screen, the recruiter sees only your face and shoulders, hears you through a microphone, and has fewer signals to read you.

  • Body language is reduced: your face, voice and posture carry almost everything.
  • Small delays make interruptions more likely, so clear pauses matter more than usual.
  • Your environment becomes part of your first impression: light, sound and background.
  • Technical problems are possible, and how you react to them is also being observed.
Tip

Think of a video interview as a short TV appearance: the content is yours, but the picture and the sound have to be good enough for people to focus on what you say.

Set up your space the day before

Do your setup the day before, at the same time of day as the interview if you can, so the light is similar. Then do not change anything.

  1. Light: face a window or place a lamp behind your screen. Avoid having a window or a bright lamp behind you, which turns your face into a silhouette.
  2. Camera height: raise your laptop on books or a box so the camera is at eye level. A camera looking up at you is unflattering and feels less engaged.
  3. Framing: leave a little space above your head and show your shoulders. You should be roughly in the center of the picture.
  4. Background: choose a tidy, neutral wall or a simple bookshelf. If you use a blurred or virtual background, test it: edges that flicker are distracting.
  5. Sound: pick the quietest room, close the window, and use wired earphones with a microphone if you have them. They usually sound clearer than a laptop microphone and avoid echo.
  6. Connection: if possible, sit close to your router or use a cable, and ask others at home to avoid heavy streaming during your slot.

Recorded (one-way) interviews

Some companies ask you to record answers to questions shown on screen, with no one on the other side. The rules vary by platform, so read the instructions carefully: how much preparation time you get, how long each answer can last, and whether you can retry.

When technology fails

Glitches happen to everyone, including recruiters. What they remember is whether you stayed calm and solved the problem. Ask for a phone number to call in case of trouble when you confirm the interview, so you have a backup plan ready.
